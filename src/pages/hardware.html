occam in silicon

occam was designed for a processor, and it later became a way to design processors. Its links now connect spacecraft. This page covers the transputer, hardware compilation with Handel-C, and the FPGA and XMOS work that continues both.

The transputer

A transputer is a microprocessor with its own RAM, a memory interface and four serial links on one chip. Links connect directly to other transputers, so you build a parallel computer by wiring chips into a grid, a pipeline, a tree or a hypercube, with no shared bus.

The instruction set supports occam directly:

  • a hardware scheduler with two priority levels runs processes, and a context switch takes about a microsecond;
  • in and out instructions perform channel communication. The same instruction works for a channel between two processes on one chip (a memory word) and a channel to another chip (a link), so a program can be spread across chips without changing its logic;
  • alt instructions implement ALT, and on-chip timers implement TIMER.

Links: IEEE 1355, SpaceWire & 4Links

The part of the transputer that has lasted longest is not the processor but its links. Today they fly in space.

OS-links

The T414 and T800 links were simple and robust. They ran at 10 or 20 Mbit/s, sent each byte in a small frame and acknowledged it, and needed only two wires each way. A link is a pair of occam channels, one in each direction. Placing a channel on a link rather than in memory was a configuration decision, not a change to the program.

DS-links and the C104

For the T9000, Inmos designed a faster link, the DS-link. It used data-strobe encoding: one wire carries the data and a second, the strobe, changes whenever the data does not. XOR-ing the two recovers the clock, so the link tolerates skew and needs no clock-recovery circuitry. DS-links carried packets that multiplexed many virtual channels over one wire. The C104 routing switch forwarded those packets between 32 links using wormhole routing, so large machines could be built from standard parts.

IEEE 1355

Inmos engineers and European partners turned DS-link technology into an open standard, IEEE Std 1355-1995, Standard for Heterogeneous InterConnect (HIC), approved on 21 September 1995. It defines single-ended, differential and fibre-optic versions running from 10 Mbit/s to 1 Gbit/s, over distances from a metre to a few kilometres. The T9000 did not succeed commercially, but its links now had a life of their own. (background)

SpaceWire

The European Space Agency needed a simple, fault-tolerant on-board network and adopted the differential DS part of IEEE 1355, with LVDS signalling and connectors fit for space. The result is SpaceWire. It is standardised as ECSS-E-ST-50-12C, and the University of Dundee's Space Technology Centre, under Steve Parkes, led the standardisation work.[source needed] It runs at 2 to 200 Mbit/s and more, and is used by ESA, NASA, JAXA and others on well over a hundred spacecraft, including the James Webb Space Telescope, Swift, the Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter and GOES-R. (overview)

SpaceWire still reflects the occam model. Flow is credit-based: a receiver sends a flow-control token for each few characters of buffer space it has free, so the sender never transmits data that cannot be accepted. That is the transputer link's acknowledgement, widened into a small window, and it is what makes a SpaceWire link behave like an occam channel pair.

4Links

4Links was founded in 2000 by former Inmos engineer Paul Walker to promote and support the link technology. Most of its customers turned out to be in the space industry. Walker contributed to the development of the SpaceWire standard, and 4Links makes SpaceWire test and development equipment: link analysers, monitors, routers and software used to integrate and test spacecraft systems. 4Links also drafted a SpaceWire Handbook for the ESA SpaceWire working group.

Handel & Handel-C

A PAR of processes that communicate over channels already looks like a circuit. Ian Page's Hardware Compilation Group at Oxford took that literally. Their 1991 paper with Wayne Luk, Compiling occam into FPGAs, described Handel, an occam subset compiled directly to gates on a field-programmable gate array.

The compilation scheme is simple:

  • each assignment takes one clock cycle, so timing is predictable from the source;
  • SEQ becomes a chain of control flip-flops that passes a "go" token along;
  • PAR starts all its branches in the same cycle and joins them when all have finished;
  • channels become handshaking circuits, and variables become registers of whatever width you declare.

Around 1996, Handel was given a C-like syntax as Handel-C. It kept par, seq, channels and prialt, and was commercialised by Embedded Solutions Ltd, later Celoxica. Handel-C was one of the first successful high-level languages for FPGA hardware/software co-design: one CSP design could be split between software on a processor and logic on an FPGA. (History)

Transputers in FPGA

Transputers are no longer made, but the design lives on in reconfigurable logic:

  • T42, a VHDL re-implementation of the T425 by Uwe Mielke and colleagues: T42 – Transputer Design in FPGA (Year Two Design Status Report), CPA 2016 (slides, paper);
  • Design of a Transputer Core and its Implementation in an FPGA;
  • Michael Brüstle's link-interface work (LINKUSB, VHDL link technical notes) on transputer.net.

Software emulators are another option. See emulators, including one that runs in your browser.

XMOS

XMOS was founded in Bristol in 2005, with transputer architect David May as a co-founder. Its xCORE processors carry the transputer's ideas into the present: hardware-scheduled threads, channel communication in the instruction set, and event-driven I/O with deterministic timing. Its XC language adds par, chan and select to C. It is the most direct commercial descendant of occam.
